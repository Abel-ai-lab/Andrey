# Score-based search

A score-based method searches for the graph that best explains the data according to a score.
We have supported Tabu search, XGES, and hill climbing since 0.3.0, which also made hill climbing
return the same graphs in less time. 0.17.0 adds AGES, for linear Gaussian data where some effects
are weak: it keeps the skeleton of the GES graph and can direct more of its edges.

Below, we explain why a search can stop at the wrong graph. We run GES, hill climbing, Tabu search,
and XGES on one small dataset, report the 0.3.0 benchmark results, and then show what AGES adds to
GES.

## Searching by a score

Andrey's score-based methods rate a graph by its BIC (Bayesian information criterion). For each
variable, the BIC measures how much variance its parents in the graph leave unexplained.
It adds a penalty for each parent. A lower score is better. This BIC assumes linear relations
with Gaussian noise. The methods also assume no hidden common causes.

The number of graphs grows faster than exponentially with the number of variables, so a search
cannot score them all. A greedy search starts from the empty graph. It takes the move that
improves the score most, such as adding, removing, or reversing one edge. It stops when no move
improves the score. That graph is a local optimum: no single move improves it. A better graph
can lie a few moves away, past graphs that score worse. A search that stops there can miss edges
or point them the wrong way.

The four methods handle local optima differently:

- **Hill climbing** searches over DAGs (directed acyclic graphs) and stops at the first local
  optimum.
- **Tabu search** takes the same moves. At a local optimum, it takes the best move even when the
  score gets worse, and keeps going. It never returns to a recently visited graph, so it cannot
  step straight back. It returns the best graph it visited, so with the same settings its score is
  never worse than hill climbing's.
- **GES** (greedy equivalence search) searches over equivalence classes: sets of graphs that fit
  any data equally well. It adds edges while the score improves, then removes edges while the
  score improves.
- **XGES** (extremely greedy equivalence search) searches the same classes with GES's moves and
  edge reversals, in a different order. Each step deletes an edge if that improves the score.
  Otherwise, it tries reversing an edge, then adding one. Its extended search then deletes each
  edge of the result in turn, forbids adding it back, and searches again. It keeps any result
  with a better score, which lets it leave a local optimum.

## Methods

Before each release, we benchmark each supported method against another package and check its
results on known graphs. Tabu search and XGES are new in 0.3.0, and AGES in 0.17.0.

| Method | Status | Searches over | Returns |
|---|---|---|---|
| [GES](../docs/api/andrey.ges.rst) | Supported | Equivalence classes | A CPDAG and its BIC score |
| [XGES](../docs/api/andrey.xges.rst) | Supported | Equivalence classes | A CPDAG and its BIC score |
| [Hill climbing](../docs/api/andrey.hc.rst) | Supported | DAGs | The CPDAG of the final DAG, and its score |
| [Tabu search](../docs/api/andrey.tabu.rst) | Supported | DAGs | The CPDAG of the best DAG visited, and its score |
| [AGES](../docs/api/andrey.ages.rst) | Supported | Equivalence classes, over a range of penalties | The GES skeleton, with the directions of the sparser graphs |
| [GIES](../docs/api/andrey.gies.rst) | Supported | Equivalence classes | A CPDAG and its BIC score; without interventions, the same search as GES |
| [Exact search](../docs/api/andrey.exact_search.rst) | Supported | Every DAG, so only a few variables | One DAG with the best BIC score |
| [CALM](../docs/api/andrey.calm.rst) | Supported | A penalized likelihood, by continuous optimization | A weighted DAG, without a score |

Choose a method for your data:

- **XGES** for continuous data that roughly fit the BIC's assumptions. In our benchmarks, its
  graphs scored better and had fewer errors than GES's (see [Benchmarks](#benchmarks)). Set
  `extended_search=False` for a faster fit that can stop at a local optimum.
- **GES** for the same data, as a well-studied reference.
- **AGES** for the same data when some effects are weak and you have many rows: it directs edges
  that GES leaves undirected, and a fit takes several times as long as one of GES (see
  [AGES](#ages)).
- **Hill climbing** as a fast baseline over DAGs, and **Tabu search** when hill climbing may stop
  early. Both take `max_iter`, a move limit.
- **Exact search** to find the best-scoring graph on a few variables.
- **CALM** to try continuous optimization that returns edge weights. You need the `torch` extra.

BOSS and GRaSP also minimize the BIC but search over orders of the variables. These are Andrey's
permutation-based methods. When hidden common causes are plausible, use the methods in the
[0.2.0 post](20261012-andrey-0-2-0.md).

## Reading a CPDAG

GES, XGES, hill climbing, Tabu search, and GIES all return a CPDAG (completed partially directed
acyclic graph). You can find its BIC score in `metadata["score"]`. AGES returns a graph tagged
`cpdag` too, but it can direct edges that the GES CPDAG leaves undirected, so it need not be the
CPDAG of one equivalence class.
A CPDAG represents an equivalence class:

- `x -> y`: every graph in the class has `x` causing `y`.
- `x -- y`: the class has graphs with `x -> y` and graphs with `x <- y`. These fit the data
  equally well, so the data cannot orient this edge.

See [Graph types](../docs/guides/graph-types.md#cpdag-an-equivalence-class) for details.

## Benchmarks

We compared XGES with xges, a Python package that implements it, and Tabu search and hill
climbing with bnlearn, an R package. The data are linear-Gaussian, simulated from random graphs
with ten rows per variable.

| Method | Compared with | Andrey's speedup | Same graph |
|---|---|---|---|
| XGES | xges | up to 140x | on every dataset |
| Tabu search | bnlearn | up to 561x | on most datasets |
| Hill climbing | bnlearn | up to 906x | on most datasets |

The [0.3.0 release card](../docs/benchmarks/0.3.0.md) reports every measurement, with the sizes,
machines, and settings. Hill climbing ran to a local optimum and Tabu search until it stopped on
its own, both with no move limit. Without the extended search, XGES still returns the same graph
as xges on every dataset, and runs faster at every size.

```{raw} html
:file: 0.17.0/speedup.html
```

On these data, XGES's graphs scored better than GES's and had fewer errors, at every size. On
the denser graphs, the extended search cut XGES's errors at every size, nearly by half at 50
variables.

Agreement between packages does not establish accuracy. From 50 variables on, Tabu search's and
hill climbing's graphs have more errors than the empty graph, in both packages. Tabu search finds
better-scoring graphs than hill climbing at every size, yet at 400 variables its graphs have more
errors. A better score is not always a better graph.

On the sparser graphs, XGES's graphs score better than the true graph at every size. With ten
rows per variable, the best-scoring graph is not the true one.
A larger `lambda_value` penalizes each parent more and favors sparser graphs.

```{raw} html
:file: 0.17.0/errors.html
```

Andrey alone also ran XGES at up to 1,000 variables, and Tabu search and hill climbing at up to
3,200. On the sparser graphs, their graphs at these sizes have more errors than the empty graph.

Speed and accuracy depend on your data, settings, and hardware.

## AGES

GES returns one CPDAG for one penalty. A higher penalty drops weak edges, and the sparser CPDAG can
direct edges that the denser one leaves undirected. AGES runs GES for every penalty from
`penalty_discount` up and overlays the directions of those CPDAGs on the CPDAG of the lowest
penalty (Eigenmann, Nandy, and Maathuis, 2017). So its graph keeps the GES skeleton, with its
missing and extra edges, and can direct more of its edges.

In 0.17.0 we compared AGES with pcalg's `ages`, the authors' implementation, on the paper's
simulation: 10 variables, 23 settings of strong and weak edges, and 100, 1,000, or 10,000 rows, with
20 datasets per setting. Andrey returned pcalg's graph on 1,378 of the 1,380 datasets, was no less
accurate in any of the 69 settings, and fit 7 to 12 times faster.

With 10,000 rows, AGES directed 49% of the true edges correctly against 46% for GES at the same
penalty, and both had the same precision, 49%: it directed more edges correctly, and more edges
wrongly, in 20 of the 23 settings. With 1,000 rows the shares were 44% against 43%, and with 100
rows there was no gain. The [0.17.0 release card](../docs/benchmarks/0.17.0.md#ages) has every
setting.

## Other highlights

- **Faster hill climbing.** Hill climbing caches the score change for each candidate move
  between steps. It recomputes only the moves a step changes.
  It returns the same graphs as before in less time.
- **Hill climbing runs in one process.** Its worker processes were slower at every measured size.
  We removed them, along with `ANDREY_HC_PARALLEL_MIN_WORK` and `andrey calibrate --method hc`.
  This change breaks scripts that set either one.
  `ANDREY_NUM_WORKERS` still sets the workers for GES, GIES, and GFCI.
- **XGES without the extended search.** `andrey.xges(data, extended_search=False)` returns the
  graph where the first search stops. On the sparser graphs at 200 variables, it took a tenth of
  the time of the full search.
- **Shorter summaries.** Since 0.2.1, a result's summary shows the length of a long metadata list
  instead of printing it in full.

For more details, read the [changelog](../docs/changelog.md).
