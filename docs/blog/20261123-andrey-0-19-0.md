# Datasets with overlapping variables

## TL;DR

- When studies, cohorts, or sites measure overlapping sets of variables, their combined table has
  whole blocks of missing values, and some pairs of variables are never observed together. A test
  of such a pair, or a test conditioned on a variable measured only in another dataset, has no rows
  to use.
- The usual answers each claim something the data did not show. Answering such a test with p = 1
  deletes true edges and creates false colliders; answering p = 0 draws an edge between every pair
  never observed together; imputation takes their covariance from a model, not from any row.
- Andrey 0.19.0 leaves such a test untested. A pair that no test decides is omitted from the graph
  and listed as undecided. An edge that an untested conditioning set might have separated is
  flagged, and one notice summarizes the result. `possibly_separable="ambiguous"` prevents a
  flagged edge from being used to infer a collider.
- On simulated cohorts of 20 to 50 variables without bridging rows, PC's graph made fewer errors
  than the empty graph in every setting and found 0.87 to 0.95 of the true edges between variables
  observed together. This was about as many as on the complete data; answering p = 1 found 0.21 to
  0.34.

---

Data often come from several datasets rather than one. A consortium pools cohorts from several
hospitals, a registry links tables collected by different agencies, and a lab combines panels run
years apart. Each dataset measures a shared core and some variables of its own. The causal
discovery literature calls these datasets with overlapping variables (Tillman et al., 2008;
Tillman and Spirtes, 2011) or datasets over overlapping variable sets (Triantafillou and
Tsamardinos, 2015).

Take two studies of four variables. Both measure `W` and `Z`; the first also measures `X`, and the
second `Y`. Here are the first rows of each:

| X | W | Z |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.13 | 0.42 | 0.95 |
| -0.13 | -0.50 | -0.31 |
| 0.64 | -0.86 | -0.24 |

| W | Z | Y |
|---:|---:|---:|
| -0.39 | 0.53 | 1.65 |
| -0.28 | 1.11 | 1.49 |
| -1.24 | -0.63 | -0.33 |

When the studies are stacked into one table, each leaves a block of missing values (`NaN`) in the
column it did not measure:

| Study | X | W | Z | Y |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 0.13 | 0.42 | 0.95 | NaN |
| 1 | -0.13 | -0.50 | -0.31 | NaN |
| 1 | 0.64 | -0.86 | -0.24 | NaN |
| 2 | NaN | -0.39 | 0.53 | 1.65 |
| 2 | NaN | -0.28 | 1.11 | 1.49 |
| 2 | NaN | -1.24 | -0.63 | -0.33 |

These missing cells are not scattered at random. They form a structured, block-wise pattern in
which some variables are never observed together (Tillman and Spirtes, 2011): here, no row observes
`X` and `Y`, so no test can determine whether they are dependent. For each test, PC and FCI use the
rows where all its variables are observed, a procedure called test-wise deletion. In this table,
some tests have no rows. How a method answers those tests determines its graph.

## A test with no rows

Consider the smallest case: two cohorts of 500 rows each from the fork `X <- Z -> Y`, one cohort
measuring `X` and `Z`, the other `Y` and `Z`. The true graph has two edges, and `X` and `Y` are
independent given `Z`. No row observes `X` and `Y` together, so no test can check that independence.
A collider `X -> Z <- Y` can give both cohorts the same distributions: these rows alone cannot
establish whether `X` and `Y` are adjacent. Every test of `X` and `Y` has no rows, as does every
test of `X` and `Z` given `Y`, or of `Y` and `Z` given `X`.

The same problem arises more generally. When some variables are never observed together, some of
their independences and dependences are unknown, so more than one graph can fit every dataset
(Tillman and Spirtes, 2011). Methods built for this setting, ION (Tillman et al., 2008), IOD
(Tillman and Spirtes, 2011), and COmbINE (Triantafillou and Tsamardinos, 2015), combine what each
dataset shows and return every graph consistent with all of them, or a summary of those graphs.
COmbINE can even infer an edge between two variables never measured together, from constraints that
the datasets impose jointly. PC and FCI read one table and do not combine datasets in this way.

## What goes wrong with the usual answers

**p = 1** treats a test with no rows as evidence of independence. It removes `X - Y`, which happens
to be correct here, and then removes the true edges `X - Z` given `Y` and `Y - Z` given `X`. In this
case, PC with p = 1 returned the empty graph on 20 of 20 seeds. With `max_depth=0`, so that no edge
is tested given a third variable, it returned the collider `X -> Z <- Y` on every seed: `X` and
`Y` were separated without `Z`, and a separation the data never showed became a collider.

**p = 0** treats a test with no rows as evidence of dependence. It keeps `X - Y`, an edge no row
supports: PC returned the triangle on 20 of 20 seeds. On larger datasets with overlapping
variables, every pair never observed together becomes an edge. Those edges create false colliders,
from about 100 to over 1,000 per fit in the benchmark below.

**Imputation**, by EM or by multiple imputation, fits a model of all columns and uses it to fill in
the missing values. No row observes `X` and `Y` together, so their covariance comes from the fitted
model, not from the data. A causal relationship learned this way between two variables never
measured together is correct only if the assumed model gets that relationship right
(Tillman et al., 2008). Andrey's `missing="em"` refuses such data and names the pair.

**Dropping** every incomplete row leaves none. Dropping `X` or `Y` until every pair shares rows
loses a variable and its edges. On the benchmark's cohorts, PC then found 0.42 to 0.75 of the true
edges between variables observed together.

## What Andrey does

- **Not tested.** A test with fewer rows than it needs (`|S| + 4` for Fisher's z, with `|S|` the
  size of the conditioning set), or with no degrees of freedom left, is not tested. It never
  separates a pair or confirms a link.
- **Undecided.** A pair that no tested query decides, such as a pair never observed together, is
  undecided. The graph omits it, and `metadata["undecided_pairs"]` lists it. No orientation treats
  it as adjacent or nonadjacent, so a triple whose ends are undecided cannot become a collider.
- **Possibly separable.** An edge is drawn when no tested query separated its pair, though an
  untested query might have. `metadata["possibly_separable"]` lists such edges. The flag indicates
  what the data could not check; it does not mean the edge is false.
- **Ambiguous, if you ask.** With `possibly_separable="ambiguous"`, which PC, FCI, CD-NOD, FCI-JCI,
  the Markov-blanket searches, and PCMCI+ accept, a triple with a flagged side is neither a
  collider nor a non-collider. The default, `"orient"`, treats it as any other triple.
- **One notice.** With the default `missing=None`, a fit on data with missing values logs one
  notice per process. It names test-wise deletion and explains when it is valid. It also counts
  the pairs never observed together, the undecided pairs, and the flagged edges, naming them when
  there are at most five.
- **Grouped sums.** Datasets with overlapping variables produce a few missingness patterns. With
  Fisher's z and Student's t, the tests compute sums from each pattern's group of rows when that
  costs less than reading every row. They also skip a conditioning set before building it if it
  contains a variable never observed with the pair. On synthetic cohorts with overlapping
  variables, `pc` ran 2.5 to 6.6 times faster with identical graphs, and with `max_depth=None` it
  finishes on 40 and 50 variables.

PC, FCI, CD-NOD, FCI-JCI, the Markov-blanket searches, PCMCI, PCMCI+, and LPCMCI leave such tests
untested, list undecided pairs, and flag possibly separable edges; the
[missing values guide](../docs/guides/missing-values.md#test-wise-deletion) gives details for each
method.

No row is complete. PC returns the true graph's CPDAG: the collider at `Z` comes from `X` and `W`,
which the first study observes together, and `Z -> Y` follows because `W` and `Y`, observed
together in the second study, are independent given `Z`. `X` and `Y` are listed as undecided and
are not drawn.

All three edges are flagged: each could have been separated by a conditioning set containing a
variable measured only by the other study, which no row can test. Here the flagged edges are true;
on larger datasets with overlapping variables, they include false edges. With `"ambiguous"`, the
collider's sides are flagged, so the graph keeps its edges but loses its arrowheads. EM refuses the
data and explains what would let it run.

## Measured on cohorts with overlapping variables

We simulated cohorts with overlapping variables using linear-Gaussian graphs with expected degree 3,
a new graph for each of 20 seeds, and columns in units from 0.1 to 100. We used three
configurations: 20 variables in 3 cohorts, 40 in 8, and 50 in 6, with 37, 144, and 134 pairs never
observed together. Each cohort measures the shared core, some modules, and a few variables of its
own. The figure shows the 20-variable configuration.

```{raw} html
:file: 0.19.0/pattern.html
```

We ran PC and FCI with Fisher's z and conditioning sets of up to 3 variables, comparing the
different answers to tests without enough rows while keeping the search the same. The table gives
PC's errors (SHD, the structural Hamming distance, averaged over seeds) with no row observing every
column and a tenth of the other values missing at random. The empty graph's errors equal the
number of true edges.

| Variables (cohorts) | Empty graph | Andrey 0.19.0 | p = 1 | p = 0 | Complete data |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 20 (3) | 29.2 | 20.1 | 28.2 | 52.9 | 8.8 |
| 40 (8) | 62.7 | 49.2 | 66.0 | 187.8 | 14.7 |
| 50 (6) | 75.8 | 56.0 | 75.6 | 190.1 | 15.8 |

- PC's and FCI's graphs made fewer errors than the empty graph in all 12 settings without rows
  observing every column, with FCI's errors counted on the skeleton. With p = 1, PC made about as
  many errors as the empty graph.
- Without bridging rows, PC found 0.87 to 0.95 of the true edges between variables observed
  together, compared with 0.88 to 0.96 on the complete data and 0.21 to 0.34 with p = 1.
- In 1,204 PC and FCI fits on data with pairs never observed together, no such pair was drawn, and
  every such pair was listed as undecided.

The [release card](../docs/benchmarks/0.19.0.md#datasets-with-overlapping-variables) reports every
setting and the intervals, along with results for dropping columns, false colliders, and time
series.

## What it costs

- **Undecided true edges are not drawn.** True edges between pairs never observed together,
  5.8 to 12.7 per fit, are listed and not drawn: no row can show them.
- **Some drawn edges are false, and the flag cannot yet tell which.** A fifth to over a third of the
  drawn edges are false. Most join variables observed together whose separating set no cohort
  observes with them. Their dependence is drawn as a direct edge. Every such edge is flagged, but
  so are 98 to 100% of all drawn edges. These edges create most of the false colliders: PC drew
  12.8 to 81.3 per fit in the table's setting, compared with 5.2 to 18.4 on the complete data.
- **"Ambiguous" trades arrowheads for safety.** It reduced PC's false colliders to at most 0.2 per
  fit and FCI's to at most 0.1, but the share of true arrowheads it found fell to 0.00. PC's graph
  then made more errors than the empty graph in all six of its settings, and FCI's skeleton errors
  rose by 0.5 to 4.4, still fewer than the empty graph's. Keep the default when you interpret
  arrowheads, and use `"ambiguous"` when a false collider costs you more than a missing one.
- **A few bridging rows decide pairs using few observations.** With 2 or 5% of the rows observing
  every column, every test has rows and the graph improves (SHD 17.6 compared with 20.1 on 20
  variables). However, a test across cohorts uses only those rows and can remove a true edge:
  with 2% bridging rows on 20 variables, PC found 0.63 of the true edges between variables
  observed together, compared with 0.87 without them.
- **Test-wise deletion assumes values missing completely at random.** Which cohort a row comes from
  is rarely random. When missingness depends on other variables, methods applied to the observed
  rows can reach wrong conclusions (Tu et al., 2019). Capping the conditioning sets with `max_depth`
  decides no pair: only rows that observe both columns do.
- **A test pools the rows of every cohort.** Each test treats the rows from all cohorts that
  observe its variables as one sample. Tillman and Spirtes (2011) report that pooling datasets in
  one test often works less well in practice than combining each dataset's p-values, as IOD does.
