# Datasets with overlapping variables

## TL;DR

- When studies, cohorts, or sites measure overlapping sets of variables, their stacked table has
  block-wise missing values, and some pairs of variables are never observed together. A
  conditional independence test of such a pair, or a test that conditions on a variable measured
  only in another dataset, has no rows to use.
- The common answers each claim something the data did not show. Answering such a test with p = 1
  deletes true edges and creates false colliders; answering p = 0 draws an edge between every pair
  never observed together; imputation takes their covariance from a model, not from any row.
- Andrey 0.19.0 skips such a test: it neither removes an edge nor keeps one. A pair that no test
  can decide is left out of the graph and listed, an edge that a skipped test might have removed is
  listed too, and one log message reports both. With `possibly_separable="ambiguous"`, such an edge
  never creates a collider.
- On simulated cohorts of 20 to 50 variables with no complete rows, PC's graph made fewer errors
  than the empty graph in every setting and found 0.87 to 0.95 of the true edges between variables
  observed together. This was about as many as on the complete data; answering p = 1 found 0.21 to
  0.34.

---

Data often come from several datasets rather than one. A consortium pools cohorts from several
hospitals, a registry links tables collected by different agencies, and a lab combines panels run
years apart. Each dataset measures some variables that the others also measure, and some of its
own. The causal discovery literature calls these datasets with overlapping variables
(Tillman et al., 2008; Tillman and Spirtes, 2011) or datasets over overlapping variable sets
(Triantafillou and Tsamardinos, 2015).

Take two studies of four variables. Both measure `W` and `Z`; the first also measures `X`, and the
second `Y`. Here are the first rows of each:

```{table}
:align: center

| X | W | Z |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.13 | 0.42 | 0.95 |
| -0.13 | -0.50 | -0.31 |
| 0.64 | -0.86 | -0.24 |
```

```{table}
:align: center

| W | Z | Y |
|---:|---:|---:|
| -0.39 | 0.53 | 1.65 |
| -0.28 | 1.11 | 1.49 |
| -1.24 | -0.63 | -0.33 |
```

When the studies are stacked into one table, each leaves a block of missing values (`NaN`) in the
column it did not measure:

```{table}
:align: center

| Study | X | W | Z | Y |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 0.13 | 0.42 | 0.95 | NaN |
| 1 | -0.13 | -0.50 | -0.31 | NaN |
| 1 | 0.64 | -0.86 | -0.24 | NaN |
| 2 | NaN | -0.39 | 0.53 | 1.65 |
| 2 | NaN | -0.28 | 1.11 | 1.49 |
| 2 | NaN | -1.24 | -0.63 | -0.33 |
```

These missing cells are not scattered at random: they are missing block-wise. Stacked datasets
leave a highly structured pattern in which some variables are never observed together (Tillman and
Spirtes, 2011). Here, no row observes `X` and `Y`, so no test can determine whether they are
dependent. For each test, PC and FCI use the rows where all its variables are observed, a procedure
called test-wise deletion. In this table, some tests have no rows. How a method answers those tests
determines its graph.

## A test with no rows

Consider the smallest case: two cohorts of 500 rows each from the fork `X <- Z -> Y`, one cohort
measuring `X` and `Z`, the other `Y` and `Z`. The true graph has two edges, and `X` and `Y` are
independent given `Z`. No row observes `X` and `Y` together, so no test can check that independence.
A collider `X -> Z <- Y` can give both cohorts the same distributions: these rows alone cannot
establish whether `X` and `Y` are adjacent. Every test of `X` and `Y` has no rows, as does every
test of `X` and `Z` given `Y`, or of `Y` and `Z` given `X`.

The same problem arises more generally. When some variables are never observed together, some of
their independences and dependences are unknown, so more than one graph can fit every dataset
(Tillman and Spirtes, 2011). Methods built for this setting, ION (Tillman et al., 2008), IOD
(Tillman and Spirtes, 2011), and COmbINE (Triantafillou and Tsamardinos, 2015), combine what each
dataset shows and return every graph consistent with all of them, or a summary of those graphs.
COmbINE can even infer an edge between two variables never measured together, from constraints that
the datasets impose jointly. PC and FCI read one table and do not combine datasets in this way.

## What goes wrong with the usual answers

**p = 1** treats a test with no rows as evidence of independence. It removes `X - Y`, which happens
to be correct here, and then removes the true edges `X - Z` given `Y` and `Y - Z` given `X`. In this
case, PC with p = 1 returned the empty graph on 20 of 20 seeds. With `max_depth=0`, so that no edge
is tested given a third variable, it returned the collider `X -> Z <- Y` on every seed: `X` and
`Y` were separated without `Z`, and a separation the data never showed became a collider.

**p = 0** treats a test with no rows as evidence of dependence. It keeps `X - Y`, an edge no row
supports: PC returned the triangle on 20 of 20 seeds. On larger datasets with overlapping
variables, every pair never observed together becomes an edge. Those edges create false colliders,
from about 100 to over 1,000 per fit in the benchmark below.

**Imputation**, by EM or by multiple imputation, fits a model of all columns and uses it to fill in
the missing values. No row observes `X` and `Y` together, so their covariance comes from the fitted
model, not from the data. After multiple imputation, a causal relationship learned between two
variables never measured together is correct only if the assumed model gets that relationship
right; Structural EM, which refits the model as it fills in the values, performed poorly on such
data (Tillman et al., 2008). Andrey's `missing="em"` refuses such data and names the pair.

**Dropping** every incomplete row leaves none. Dropping `X` or `Y` until every pair shares rows
loses a variable and its edges. On the benchmark's cohorts, PC then found 0.42 to 0.75 of the true
edges between variables observed together.

## What Andrey does

- **A test without enough rows is skipped.** A conditional independence test with fewer rows than
  it needs (`|S| + 4` for Fisher's z, with `|S|` the size of the conditioning set), or with no
  degrees of freedom left, is skipped. A skipped test never removes an edge and never counts as
  evidence of dependence.
- **A pair no test can decide is left out.** When no test with rows decides a pair, as for a pair
  never observed together, the graph leaves the pair out and `metadata["undecided_pairs"]` lists
  it. No orientation rule reads it as adjacent or as nonadjacent, so a triple whose two ends form
  such a pair never becomes a collider.
- **An edge a skipped test might have removed is listed.** An edge is drawn when no test with rows
  separated its pair, although a skipped test, whose conditioning set was never observed with the
  pair, might have. `metadata["possibly_separable"]` lists such edges. The list shows what the data
  could not check; it does not mean the edge is false.
- **Such edges can be kept out of colliders.** With `possibly_separable="ambiguous"`, which PC, FCI,
  CD-NOD, FCI-JCI, the Markov-blanket searches, and PCMCI+ accept, a triple with one of these edges
  as a side is neither a collider nor a non-collider. The default, `"orient"`, orients it like any
  other triple.
- **One log message.** With the default `missing=None`, a fit on data with missing values logs one
  message per process. It names test-wise deletion and says when it is valid. It also counts the
  pairs never observed together, the pairs left out, and the listed edges, naming them when there
  are at most five.
- **Faster tests on few missingness patterns.** Datasets with overlapping variables produce few
  missingness patterns. With Fisher's z and Student's t, a test sums the rows of each pattern once
  when that costs less than reading every row. It also skips, before building it, a conditioning
  set that contains a variable never observed with the pair. On synthetic cohorts with overlapping
  variables, `pc` ran 2.5 to 6.6 times faster with identical graphs, and with `max_depth=None` it
  finishes on 40 and 50 variables.

PC, FCI, CD-NOD, FCI-JCI, the Markov-blanket searches, PCMCI, PCMCI+, and LPCMCI skip such tests
and keep both lists; the
[missing values guide](../docs/guides/missing-values.md#test-wise-deletion) gives details for each
method.

No row is complete. PC returns the true graph's CPDAG: the collider at `Z` comes from `X` and `W`,
which the first study observes together, and `Z -> Y` follows because `W` and `Y`, observed
together in the second study, are independent given `Z`. `X` and `Y` are left out of the graph and
listed in `undecided_pairs`.

All three edges are listed in `possibly_separable`: each could have been removed by a conditioning
set containing a variable measured only by the other study, which no row can test. Here these edges
are true; on larger datasets with overlapping variables, the list includes false edges. With
`possibly_separable="ambiguous"`, both sides of the collider are on the list, so the graph keeps
its edges but loses its arrowheads. EM refuses the data and explains what would let it run.

## Measured on cohorts with overlapping variables

We simulated cohorts with overlapping variables using linear-Gaussian graphs with expected degree 3,
a new graph for each of 20 seeds, and columns in units from 0.1 to 100. We used three
configurations: 20 variables in 3 cohorts, 40 in 8, and 50 in 6, with 37, 144, and 134 pairs never
observed together. Some variables are measured by every cohort, some by several cohorts, and a few
by one cohort only. The figure shows the missingness pattern of the 20-variable configuration.

```{raw} html
:file: 0.19.0/pattern.html
```

We ran PC and FCI with Fisher's z and conditioning sets of up to 3 variables, comparing the
different answers to tests without enough rows while keeping the search the same. The table gives
PC's errors (SHD, the structural Hamming distance, averaged over seeds) with no row observing every
column and a tenth of the other values missing at random. The empty graph's errors equal the
number of true edges.

| Variables (cohorts) | Empty graph | Andrey 0.19.0 | p = 1 | p = 0 | Complete data |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 20 (3) | 29.2 | 20.1 | 28.2 | 52.9 | 8.8 |
| 40 (8) | 62.7 | 49.2 | 66.0 | 187.8 | 14.7 |
| 50 (6) | 75.8 | 56.0 | 75.6 | 190.1 | 15.8 |

- PC's and FCI's graphs made fewer errors than the empty graph in all 12 settings without rows
  observing every column, with FCI's errors counted on the skeleton. With p = 1, PC made about as
  many errors as the empty graph.
- With no complete rows, PC found 0.87 to 0.95 of the true edges between variables observed
  together, compared with 0.88 to 0.96 on the complete data and 0.21 to 0.34 with p = 1.
- In 1,204 PC and FCI fits on data with pairs never observed together, no such pair was drawn, and
  every such pair was listed in `undecided_pairs`.

The [release card](../docs/benchmarks/0.19.0.md#datasets-with-overlapping-variables) reports every
setting and the intervals, along with results for dropping columns, false colliders, and time
series.

## What it costs

- **True edges between pairs never observed together are not drawn.** There were 5.8 to 12.7 per
  fit. They are listed, not drawn: no row can show them.
- **Some drawn edges are false, and the list cannot yet tell which.** A fifth to over a third of the
  drawn edges are false. Most join variables observed together whose separating set no cohort
  observes with them. Their dependence is drawn as a direct edge. Every such edge is in
  `possibly_separable`, but so are 98 to 100% of all drawn edges. These edges create most of the
  false colliders: PC drew 12.8 to 81.3 per fit in the table's setting, compared with 5.2 to 18.4 on
  the complete data.
- **`possibly_separable="ambiguous"` trades arrowheads for fewer false colliders.** It reduced PC's
  false colliders to at most 0.2 per fit and FCI's to at most 0.1, but the share of true arrowheads
  it found fell to 0.00. PC's graph then made more errors than the empty graph in all six of its
  settings, and FCI's skeleton errors rose by 0.5 to 4.4, still fewer than the empty graph's. Keep
  the default when you interpret arrowheads, and use `"ambiguous"` when a false collider costs you
  more than a missing one.
- **A few complete rows decide pairs from few observations.** With 2 or 5% of the rows complete,
  every test has rows and the graph improves (SHD 17.6 compared with 20.1 on 20 variables). However,
  a test across cohorts uses only those rows and can remove a true edge: with 2% complete rows on 20
  variables, PC found 0.63 of the true edges between variables observed together, compared with 0.87
  without them.
- **Test-wise deletion assumes values missing completely at random.** Which cohort a row comes from
  is rarely random. When missingness depends on other variables, methods applied to the observed
  rows can reach wrong conclusions (Tu et al., 2019). Capping the conditioning sets with `max_depth`
  decides no pair: only rows that observe both columns do.
- **A test pools the rows of every cohort.** Each test treats the rows from all cohorts that
  observe its variables as one sample. Tillman and Spirtes (2011) note that pooling datasets in
  one test often works less well in practice than combining each dataset's p-values, as IOD does.
